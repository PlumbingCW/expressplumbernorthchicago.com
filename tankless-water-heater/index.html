Tankless Water Heater in North Chicago, IL

A tankless unit heats water as it flows, so it never runs out the way a tank can. It also asks more of your home: a bigger gas line, a stainless vent, and a yearly flush. We install, flush and service tankless water heaters across North Chicago.

Most tankless work is planned, and we offer same-day appointments when available. If a tankless unit is leaking onto the floor, shut its cold water valve and call. Urgent leaks are handled 24/7.

1

What a tankless unit needs from your house

The surprise with tankless is rarely the heater. It is the gas line. A unit that fires hard for a long shower can draw several times what a tank burner does, so the pipe from the meter has to carry it without starving other appliances.

We check four things before quoting: the gas pipe size and length, the vent path to the outside, the water pressure and flow at your busiest fixture, and where the condensate drains. High-efficiency units make acidic condensate that needs a proper drain, not a floor.

What we measure on the first visit

  • ✓Gas supply. Pipe diameter, run length and the meter's rated capacity.
  • ✓Vent route. Distance to an outside wall or roof, and clearances to windows.
  • ✓Water demand. How many fixtures run at once in your household.
  • ✓Drain and power. Where condensate goes and whether an outlet is within reach.
2

Lake Michigan water and what it leaves behind

North Chicago's tap water comes from Lake Michigan, treated at the city's own plant on Lakeview Drive. The city's water quality reports do not publish a hardness number, and hardness is what drives scale inside a tankless heat exchanger. That means the safest plan is to treat scale as a maintenance item, not a rumor.

In practice we ask you to test your tap with a hardness kit or read the city's latest report, and then set a flush schedule from what you find. If scale turns out to be heavy, a water softener or filter ahead of the heater protects the warranty and the flow.

3

Flushing and descaling a tankless heater

This is the most common tankless service call, and it is planned, not urgent.

Over time, minerals coat the heat exchanger and the water flow narrows. You may see lukewarm water, a unit that shuts off mid-shower, or an error code that comes back after a reset. A flush pushes a descaling solution through the unit and rinses it clean.

  1. 1

    Isolate the unit

    We close the water and gas supplies and open the service valves, which most installs include.

  2. 2

    Circulate descaler

    A pump moves a mild acid solution through the heat exchanger for a set time.

  3. 3

    Rinse and clean the filter

    We flush with clean water and clear the small inlet screen that catches grit.

  4. 4

    Restart and confirm

    We bring the unit back up, check the error history and test hot water at a fixture.

If your unit has no service valves, say so when you call. We can add them, and they make every later flush faster.

4

Tankless versus a standard tank in an older house

North Chicago's median house was built in 1973, and about a third of the housing was built before 1960. Older homes often have a small gas line, a chimney flue built for a tank, and tight utility closets. Switching to tankless in these homes can mean new piping and a new vent, which changes the price more than the heater does.

5

When your tankless heater stops working

Error codes on tankless units usually point to ignition, flow, venting or a blocked condensate line. Some can be cleared by a reset, but a code that returns tells you something is wrong. If the unit is leaking, close its water valves and call.

Tell us the brand, the code on the display and what you noticed first. A unit that makes a loud booming sound on startup or shows a vent error needs a visit, not another reset. For other no-hot-water problems, see water heater repair.

What decides the price of a tankless job

What changes the price

Two tankless jobs on the same street can cost very different amounts. These are the drivers.

Unit capacity and brandHigher flow ratings and condensing models cost more.
Gas line upgradeA longer or thicker supply pipe adds material and labor.
VentingSidewall venting is simpler than a long run through floors or the roof.
Condensate and powerA drain and an outlet nearby keep the job short.
Service valves and filtersAdding isolation valves now makes later flushes cheaper.
Insurance seldom pays for planned upgrades. If a leak damaged your home first, your carrier may cover that damage.
