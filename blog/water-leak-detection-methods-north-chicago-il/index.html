Water Leak Detection Methods: North Chicago, IL Guide

Water leak detection methods run from a five-minute meter check you can do yourself to acoustic listening and pressure testing done by a plumber. The best choice depends on where the water is going: behind a wall, under a slab, or in the buried line between the street and your house.

Short answer: start with the meter test. If the dial moves while every fixture is off, water is escaping somewhere past the meter, and a plumber narrows it down with pressure testing, listening gear or thermal imaging. If water is spraying right now, shut the main valve first and read later.

1

The five-minute meter test

Every other method on this page is easier once you know whether a leak exists at all.

Your water meter counts every gallon that enters the building. If the count keeps climbing when nothing is running, the water is going somewhere you cannot see. The test costs nothing and takes about as long as a cup of coffee.

  1. 1

    Stop every use of water

    Turn off the dishwasher, washing machine, ice maker and humidifier. Make sure no one flushes a toilet during the test.

  2. 2

    Read the meter

    Lift the cover and look for the small triangle or wheel, often called the low-flow indicator. Note its position or write down the full number.

  3. 3

    Wait 15 to 30 minutes

    Do not touch any tap. A longer wait catches slow leaks that a short one misses.

  4. 4

    Read it again

    If the wheel turned or the number moved, you have a leak. If it did not move, check for a leak that only runs while a fixture is in use, such as a toilet that refills on its own.

One more check separates a leak inside the house from one in the buried line. Close the main shutoff valve inside your home, then look at the meter again. If it still moves with the house valve closed, the loss is in the pipe between the meter and that valve.

2

Seven methods and what each one actually finds

No single tool finds every leak. A good plumber picks the method to match the symptom, and often combines two. This table shows where each one earns its place and where it falls short.

Leak detection methods compared
MethodBest at findingWhere it falls short
Meter testWhether any leak exists on the propertyCannot say where the leak is
Pressure testLeaks in a section of supply pipe, by isolating it and watching for a pressure dropNeeds shutoffs to isolate zones, so it narrows a leak to a section rather than a spot
Acoustic listeningPinhole leaks and pressurized breaks in supply lines, including buried onesPlastic pipe and noisy surroundings muffle the sound
Thermal imagingHot-water leaks and wet insulation behind drywall or under floorsCold-water leaks show faintly, and the camera sees surfaces only
Moisture meterDamp drywall, subfloor and framing around a suspected spotConfirms wetness but not the source, which may be several feet away
Dye or tracer testToilet tank leaks and drain connections that seep only under useDoes not help with pressurized supply lines
Camera inspectionCracked or broken drain and sewer linesDrain pipes only, so supply leaks are invisible to it

Notice that the cheapest tools come first. A plumber who opens a wall before running a pressure test or listening along the pipe is guessing. We would rather confirm the location, then cut a small access hole exactly where it is needed.

3

Match the method to what you are seeing

A warm patch on the floor

Heat on a slab or tile points to a hot-water line under the floor. Thermal imaging and a pressure test on the hot side usually settle it.

A water bill that jumped

Run the meter test first. A moving dial with everything off means a hidden leak, and a pressure test shows whether it is inside or outside.

Running water you can hear at night

Acoustic listening is the right tool here. The sound often travels along the pipe, so the loudest spot is not always the leak.

A brown stain on a ceiling

Water travels downhill and sideways before it shows. A moisture meter and thermal camera trace the stain back to the pipe above it.

If your symptom sounds like more than one of these, that is normal. Leaks rarely read the textbook, and our leak detection visits usually start with the meter and a quick look at every fixture before any equipment comes out.

4

Why older North Chicago buildings hide leaks differently

The median North Chicago home was built in 1973, and 32.3% of homes date from before 1960. Pipes from the older group are more likely to be galvanized steel or early copper, and galvanized lines corrode from the inside out. That tends to produce pinholes and weak elbows rather than one dramatic burst, which is why we check fittings and bends first.

About 67.4% of households here rent, and many live in multi-unit buildings. In those buildings a leak may start in a neighbor's unit or in a shared riser. If you rent, report the stain and the meter reading to your landlord in writing, take dated photos, and keep water away from outlets until someone has looked.

The city supplies its own water from a Lake Michigan plant, and it is the city meter that records every gallon your building uses. That makes the meter test described above a reliable first step in any North Chicago house or apartment building.

5

When the leak is in the buried line

The service line runs from the water main to your meter and into the house. It sits underground, so the signs are indirect: a soft or unusually green patch of lawn in dry weather, a sudden drop in pressure, or a meter that spins with the house valve closed. Listening gear pinpoints the break so only a short trench is dug.

Fixing it is a different job from finding it. A cracked or corroded line may be repaired in one spot or replaced, and our water service line repair work starts with a confirmed location and a clear price before digging begins. In Illinois, a JULIE call (811) comes before any digging so buried utilities get marked.

Signs the leak is outside the house

  • ✓The meter moves with the main valve inside the house closed
  • ✓A wet or sunken patch above the line path with no rain
  • ✓Pressure that dropped at every tap at once
  • ✓Water noise at the meter or where the pipe enters the basement

Notes for neighbors in Libertyville and Lake Bluff

We also trace leaks for homeowners in Libertyville and Lake Bluff, a few miles from the 3100 Skokie Highway shop where our trucks dispatch. Each village runs its own distribution system, and the two handle service lines in ways that change who calls whom.

Local note

Libertyville and Lake Bluff: who owns which pipe

Lake Bluff states that the entire water service line is owned by the homeowner, and that maintenance and repair of the private line is the homeowner's responsibility. If your meter shows a leak between the street and the house, plan on hiring the repair yourself rather than waiting for the Village.

The Village also notes that when it upgraded meters between 2019 and 2021, it identified probable service line materials and wrote to properties with suspected or known lead lines. If you did not get a letter, the Village says it does not suspect lead in your line.

Libertyville has bought Lake Michigan water through the Central Lake County Joint Action Water Agency since May 1992, and the Village maintains its own distribution system. It has published a Water Service Material Inventory as an online dashboard and offers a voluntary cost-sharing program for lead line replacement. The Village says lead lines were commonly installed before World War II, so a leak repair on an older Libertyville property is a good time to ask what material your line is.

Sources: Village of Lake Bluff lead service line page and water quality report, Village of Libertyville water and lead service line pages.
