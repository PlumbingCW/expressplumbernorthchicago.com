Does a Tankless Water Heater Use Gas? North Chicago Guide

Does a tankless water heater use gas? Yes, many of them do, and many others run on electricity. The fuel is a choice you make when you buy, not a feature of tankless itself. In North Chicago, IL homes with gas service, a gas tankless unit is the common pick, but it asks more of your house than a standard tank does.

The short version: a gas tankless heater burns a lot of fuel in short bursts, so it needs a gas line that can deliver enough volume, a vent that can carry the exhaust safely out, and a licensed installer who tests the connections. This guide covers what each fuel type needs and what to confirm in Gurnee and Lake Forest before you order.

1

Gas or electric: how the two types compare

A tankless unit has no storage tank. When you open a hot tap, flow sensors start a heat source that warms water as it passes through. That source is either a gas burner or electric elements. Both give hot water on demand. They differ in what they ask from the building.

Gas and electric tankless units side by side

Fuel supplyNatural gas or propane through a gas line sized for the unitElectric power through dedicated high-amp breakers

Notice the last row. A gas tankless unit does not work in a power outage unless it has a backup, because the fan and controls are electric. A standard gas tank with a pilot can keep making hot water when the lights go out. That detail catches people off guard.

2

Why gas capacity decides the whole job

A gas tank heater sips fuel while it keeps a tank warm. A gas tankless heater has to heat a continuous stream of cold water to shower temperature in seconds, so its burner draws far more gas at the moment it fires, often several times what a tank pulls. Your meter, your main gas line, and the branch to the heater all have to carry that load while the furnace and range are running too.

If the existing pipe is undersized, the fix is a larger line, which is real piping work behind walls or across a basement ceiling. That is why we look at the gas run before we talk about equipment. Our tankless water heater page lists what we examine on a site visit.

Safety first: gas piping is not a DIY job

We do not give step-by-step instructions for connecting or modifying gas lines, and you should not attempt it. A bad fitting can leak without any visible sign. Use a licensed plumber, and expect the work to be pressure tested before the unit is lit.

If you ever smell gas, do not flip switches or look for the source. Get everyone out, then call your gas utility or 911 from outside.

3

Venting: where the exhaust goes

Every gas heater must send its exhaust outdoors. Older tank heaters often share a masonry chimney. Tankless models usually cannot, because their exhaust is cooler and wetter, and the old flue can collect moisture and corrode. High-efficiency condensing units use plastic vent pipe that runs out through a wall or roof, and they drain acidic condensate that must go to a proper drain.

The route matters. A vent that must run twenty feet with several turns is a different job than one that exits a wall three feet away. Clearances from windows, meters, and property lines are set by the manufacturer and the local code, so the installer needs to see the room before pricing anything.

4

Questions to settle before you order

  1. 1

    Pick the fuel that your house already supports

    If gas service is in the building, gas is likely the easier path. If not, an electric unit may need a service upgrade, which is a larger project than the heater.

  2. 2

    Find the gas line size and the meter capacity

    Ask the installer to confirm both. This is the step that people skip and regret.

  3. 3

    Walk the vent route with the installer

    Agree on where the exhaust leaves the building and where the condensate will drain.

  4. 4

    Decide on water treatment

    Mineral scale shortens the life of a heat exchanger. Ask about isolation valves that make flushing easy, and about a scale filter if your water leaves deposits.

  5. 5

    Confirm the permit path

    Ask the building department what your town requires for a water heater, and who pulls the permit.

For a straight swap of a gas tank to a gas tankless unit, our water heater installation crew handles the plumbing, the gas connection, and the testing as one visit when the gas line and vent are already suitable.

5

Gurnee and Lake Forest: what the local pages say

Local note

Gurnee and Lake Forest rules and water facts that touch a tankless install

The Village of Gurnee handles water heaters through its Building Safety Division. The village's water heater page asks for the name of a licensed plumber, who must be registered with the village before the permit is released, plus a written letter of intent from that plumber, on company letterhead and signed, notarized or carrying a corporate seal. A tankless installation in Gurnee follows that path, so the schedule should leave time for it.

On water quality, the village describes Gurnee's water as moderately hard, at approximately 8 grains per gallon or 137 milligrams per liter as calcium carbonate. Harder water leaves scale on hot surfaces, which is exactly what a tankless heat exchanger is. That is the practical reason we talk about flush valves and routine descaling with Gurnee customers.

The City of Lake Forest lists a residential Water Heater Permit on its building permits page and adopts the Illinois Plumbing Code with local changes, along with the International Fuel Gas Code. The page does not describe when the permit is required, so confirm with the Community Development department. Lake Forest does not publish a hardness figure that we could find, so we say nothing about it and suggest testing your tap.

If you live in the village, our Gurnee plumbing page explains how we work there. For the city, see Lake Forest plumbing service, a short drive south from our Skokie Highway address.

Tankless and gas: straight answers

Either. Gas models burn natural gas or propane and also use a small amount of electricity for the controls and fan. Electric models use only electricity and draw a large current. Which one fits depends on your service, your panel, and your gas line.
