Basement Waterproofing for North Chicago, IL Homes

Basement and crawl space waterproofing in North Chicago starts with a question, not a product: where is the water coming from? A wet wall, a seeping floor joint, a failed sump pump and a sewer backup all put water in the same room, and each one needs a different fix.

This guide helps you work out which kind of wet you have, compares interior and exterior approaches, and explains why drainage near the Skokie Highway corridor and the Skokie River makes it worth getting the diagnosis right the first time.

1

Four ways water gets in, and how to tell them apart

Spend ten minutes looking before anyone sells you a system.

  • 1Condensation.. Moisture on cold pipes, ductwork or the inside of concrete walls in humid weather. Tape a square of foil to the wall for a day. If the water forms on the room side, it is condensation, not a leak.
  • 2Seepage through walls or the floor joint.. Damp, stained or white powdery patches that grow after rain. Water at the joint where the wall meets the slab points to groundwater pressure.
  • 3Pump or drain failure.. Water rising from a pit or floor drain, with a pump that has stopped or cannot keep up. This is a repair question before it is a waterproofing question.
  • 4Sewer or drain backup.. Water that smells and comes up through floor drains or a toilet. That is a drain-line problem, and no wall coating will stop it.

Write down when it happens. Water that shows up only after heavy rain points to the ground and gutters. Water that shows up on a dry day points to a leaking pipe or a high water table, and a plumber should look at it.

2

Fix the outside before you spend inside

The cheapest waterproofing is moving water away from the foundation. Downspouts should discharge several feet from the wall, and the soil around the house should slope away, not toward it. Gutters packed with leaves send roof water straight down the foundation.

These steps will not cure a high water table, but they cut the load on any pump or drain system you add later. Do them first. If a contractor proposes a large interior system without asking about your gutters, grading or downspouts, ask why.

Interior systems are the most common choice because they work from inside and avoid digging around the house. Exterior work can be the right answer on a wall that leaks in one place, but it requires a call to 811 before any digging, and utilities can restrict where you dig. Our basement waterproofing page explains how we approach the choice, and a pit with a weak pump is a job for sump pump repair.

4

Crawl spaces are a different animal

We do not have a sourced figure for how many North Chicago homes have basements, slabs or crawl spaces, so we will not guess. What we can say is how a crawl space behaves. Bare earth floors release moisture upward all year, and a vented crawl space brings in humid summer air that condenses on cool surfaces.

Signs of trouble are a musty smell upstairs, sagging insulation, visible mold on joists, and standing water after storms. The usual fixes are a ground vapor barrier, controlled drainage with a small pump, and sealing the walls against moist air. Before any of that, check that plumbing in the crawl space is not leaking, because a weeping pipe fools many people into buying drainage they do not need.

5

Questions to ask before you sign anything

Where is the water entering?

A contractor who cannot point to the entry spot is guessing. Ask to be shown.

What happens when the power goes out?

A drain system that depends on a pump needs backup power. Ask what is included.

Where does the water discharge?

It must go somewhere that is allowed and well away from the house. Ask who checked.

Who handles permits and locates?

Exterior digging needs an 811 request. Local work may need a permit. Ask who files it.

Water, flooding and sewers: what the records show

Local note

North Chicago's documented flood history and the drainage project

The Lake County watershed plan ties North Chicago to the July 11 and 12, 2017 storm by name. A post-event survey found 10 flood problem areas and 23 properties affected, including three sewer backups.

The same plan reports that the Strawberry Condominiums had more than 50 residential units with first-floor flood damage in each of two incidents between 2013 and 2019. It also says the Skokie River and Route 41 corridor has a history of damage and road closures tied to undersized culverts.

Lake County's FEMA projects page lists a North Chicago Storm Sewer Project under a FY2022 hazard mitigation grant, worth several million dollars in total, to replace undersized storm sewers and improve the Skokie River culvert and channel.

FEMA's public notice says the aim is to reduce flood risk along Skokie Highway, at the Strawberry Condominiums and at Navy Housing. The North Shore Water Reclamation District states that sanitary and storm sewers in its area are separate systems with no combined sewers.
