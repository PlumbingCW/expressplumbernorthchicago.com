Under-Sink Water Filters for North Chicago, IL Homes

A water filtration system for the sink does one job: it cleans the water at the single faucet where you drink and cook. For most North Chicago homes the best match is an under-sink filter with a certified carbon block, and a reverse osmosis unit if you want to cut dissolved minerals and metals too.

Below you will find how each type works, what to check on the label, how to read the city's water quality report, and what the city's lead service line records mean for your choice.

1

Three kinds of under-sink filter, side by side

The names on the box matter less than the certification printed on it.

Carbon block filter

Cleans up chlorine taste and smell and traps some particles. Simple, low cost to run, and the usual first choice for city water that tastes of chlorine. Cartridges are changed on a schedule.

Carbon plus lead-rated cartridge

Adds a media rated for lead and certain other metals. Worth a look if your home has an older service line or older solder joints. The label must say lead reduction, not just filtration.

Reverse osmosis

Pushes water through a fine membrane into a small storage tank and gives it its own faucet. Cuts more dissolved material, but needs more space, a drain connection and membrane changes.

None of these makes water sterile, and none replaces the city's treatment. They are point-of-use tools. The goal is cleaner water at the tap you use for drinking, coffee and cooking, which is a tiny fraction of the water your home uses.

2

Read the label before you buy

Look for NSF/ANSI certification marks on the box and the cartridge. Standard 42 covers taste and odor such as chlorine. Standard 53 covers health-related reductions such as lead. A claim that is not backed by one of these marks is just marketing.

Check the replacement cartridge cost and how often you must swap it. A cheap housing with an expensive cartridge costs more over a few years than a pricier unit with a common cartridge. Also check the flow rate, since a filter that fills a glass slowly gets switched off and ignored.

3

What North Chicago's water report actually says

North Chicago draws its water from Lake Michigan through two intakes and treats it at the city plant on Lakeview Drive. The most recent report posted on the city site covers 2023. It lists chlorine as the disinfectant, with a highest running average of 1.4 ppm against a limit of 4 ppm and no violation. That chlorine is the taste most people notice, and a carbon block handles it well.

The same report shows fluoride at 0.6 ppm and sodium at 15 ppm. It does not print a hardness number. We will not guess one for you. If scale on your kettle and fixtures bothers you, buy a cheap hardness test strip kit and test your own tap, because a filter is the wrong tool for hardness while a softener is the right one.

PFAS testing is posted on a separate city page. If that is your concern, read the lab sheet, then look for a filter whose certification names PFAS reduction. Do not assume a basic carbon cartridge covers it.

4

Lead: the reason many North Chicago homes should ask about the line

The city's lead service line plan lists 5,653 service lines. Of those, 849 are lead and 144 are galvanized lines that need replacement. A lead filter is a sensible stopgap while that is sorted out, but it is not a substitute for knowing what runs from the street to your meter.

Look where the pipe enters your basement, just before the meter. Lead is dull gray and soft enough to scratch with a key, and a magnet will not stick to it. Do not scrape hard. If you are not sure, a plumber can identify it in minutes. Replacing a failing or lead service pipe is the job of water service line repair, and it is a different project from picking a faucet filter.

About two thirds of households in the city rent. If you rent, ask your landlord what material your service line is and tell them in writing if you want a lead-rated filter. If you own, the city is replacing lead lines at no cost in one defined area, so check whether yours is included before paying for private work.

How we install one, start to finish

  1. 1

    Check the cabinet and shutoffs

    We look at the supply valves, the drain, and whether a disposal leaves room under the sink.

  2. 2

    Shut off and tap in

    Water to the sink is closed, the cold supply gets a tee or a dedicated line, and the housing is mounted to the cabinet wall.

  3. 3

    Add the faucet

    A dedicated filtered faucet goes in a spare sink hole or a drilled countertop hole, with tubing run to the unit.

  4. 4

    Flush and test

    We flush new cartridges as the maker directs, check every fitting for drips, and run water at full flow.

  5. 5

    We write the cartridge change date on the housing so you are not guessing in six months.

Two neighboring towns, two different answers

Do not copy a neighbor's filter choice without checking whose water they drink. Waukegan runs its own plant, and the city says lead is not present in its treated water, though the city still runs a lead service line replacement program. Hardness is not printed in what we could read.

In Lake Bluff, the village buys its water from the Central Lake County Joint Action Water Agency, and the agency adds phosphate to keep lead and copper out of home plumbing. The agency's own FAQ gives hardness as about 8 grains per gallon, which is moderately hard. Same lake, different supply, different tap results.

7

What a sink filter will not do

It will not soften your whole house, protect your water heater from scale, or fix a rusty service line. It will not remove everything the label does not name. And it will make water worse if the cartridge goes unchanged for a year. If your real problem is scale or a corroding line, tell us, and we will point you to the right fix instead of selling a filter.

8

What we can verify about North Chicago water

Local note

North Chicago water, from the city's own records

The city's published water quality report for 2023 lists Lake Michigan as the source, with chlorine as the disinfectant. It reports a lead 90th percentile of 15 ppb, which sits at the federal action level, with three sampling sites over it. Copper came in at 0.046 ppm with no sites over the limit.

The city's lead service line replacement plan, dated September 2024, counts 849 lead lines and 144 galvanized lines needing replacement out of 5,653.

The city page says it is replacing lead lines at no cost to owners in eligible properties east of Green Bay Road, south of Argonne Road and north of Broadway Avenue, with Illinois EPA grant funding. A large part of the city is Navy property served by the Navy's own water system, so these figures do not describe base housing.
