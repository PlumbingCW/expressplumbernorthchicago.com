Sump Pump Repair in North Chicago, IL: Signs and Fixes

Sump pump repair in North Chicago usually comes down to one of four things: a stuck float, a clogged intake, a dead motor, or a power problem. If your pump runs constantly, never runs, or hums without moving water, fix it before the next hard rain, not during it.

This guide shows the signs of a failing pump, a quick test you can do with a bucket, and how to decide between a repair and a swap. It also covers what the public flood records around North Chicago say about why basements here need a pump that works on the worst day.

1

Seven signs the pump is on its way out

Most pumps give a warning before they quit. You just have to be standing in the basement to notice it.

  • ✓It runs all the time.. A pump that never shuts off may have a float stuck in the up position, or it may be undersized for the water coming in. Either way, it is wearing out fast.
  • ✓It never runs at all.. Pour water into the pit. If nothing happens, check the outlet and the breaker first, then the float. A dead pump in a dry week still needs attention before the weather turns.
  • ✓It hums but moves no water.. That sound usually means a seized impeller or a jammed check valve. Grit and small stones from the pit are common culprits.
  • ✓It cycles every few seconds.. Short cycling points to a float switch that is badly placed, or a check valve that lets water fall back into the pit after each run.
  • ✓Loud grinding or rattling.. Bearings or a damaged impeller. These get louder, not quieter.
  • ✓Rust, silt or a musty smell in the pit.. Mud buildup shortens motor life and can jam the float. A pit that has never been cleaned is a pit that will fail early.
  • ✓The pump is old and you do not know how old.. If you moved in as a renter or just bought, ask whether anyone has ever serviced it. Unknown age is a reason to test it, not to trust it.

None of these need a plumber on the first step. A few minutes with a flashlight tells you whether you have a simple clog or a pump that is finished.

3

A five-minute pump test with a bucket

Do this when the weather is dry, not when the basement is already damp.

  1. 1

    Look before you touch.

    Check that the pump is plugged into a working outlet and that the cord is not frayed. If the outlet is a GFCI, press the reset button.

  2. 2

    Clear the pit surface.

    Remove the lid if there is one. Lift out any debris you can see, like gravel, a stray rag or a leaf mat. Wear gloves.

  3. 3

    Fill the pit.

    Pour about five gallons slowly into the pit from a bucket. Watch the float. It should rise and trigger the pump.

  4. 4

    Listen and watch the discharge.

    The pump should start, move the water out in a few seconds, and shut off cleanly. Water should leave through the discharge pipe, not splash back.

  5. 5

    Repeat once.

    A second run catches a float that sticks only after it has settled. If either run fails, stop and call. Do not reach into the pit with the pump plugged in.

If the pump passes, note the date. A pump that passes today can still fail in the next storm, so test it again each spring and after any long dry spell.

4

Repair it or replace it

A float switch, a check valve or a clogged screen is a cheap, quick repair on a pump that is otherwise sound. A failed motor, a cracked housing or a pump that has been replaced once already usually points to a new unit. The labor is about the same either way, so a new pump on an old, tired motor often costs less over time than patching.

We will tell you which one you have after looking, and we do not sell a replacement when a ten-minute fix will do. The price is clear before work starts. If the pit is cracked, undersized or full of silt, we will say so, since a new pump in a bad pit fails for the same reasons the old one did.

Our sump pump repair work covers the pump, the float, the check valve and the discharge line. If water keeps coming in faster than any pump should have to handle, the real fix may be outside the pump, which is where basement waterproofing comes in.

5

Backup power and discharge, the two things people skip

Power fails when the storm hits

The storms that fill a pit are the same storms that knock out electricity. A pump with no backup is a pump that stops at the worst moment. A battery backup unit sits beside the main pump and takes over when the power drops or the main pump fails. A water-powered backup is another route, but it depends on your water pressure and has its own limits, so ask before choosing one.

Where the water goes matters

A pump that moves water ten feet and drops it beside the foundation just recycles it into the pit. The discharge should carry water well away from the house and away from the neighbor's property. A line that sags can hold water and freeze in winter, so it should slope steadily and exit where it can drain freely.

Census figures put the median North Chicago home at about 1973, and roughly a third of the housing stock was built before 1960. Older foundations and older drain tile are more likely to leak at the walls or floor joint, so the pump works harder. About two out of three households here rent, which means many pumps belong to a landlord and a basement may be shared by several units.

If you rent, tell your landlord in writing when the pump fails or starts acting strange. If you own a multi-unit building, check the pit and the cord on a schedule. We can service the equipment either way, but the owner is the one who decides on repair or replacement.

What the public flood records say about basements near North Chicago

Local note

North Chicago, Waukegan and Lake Bluff: what the published records show

The Lake County watershed plan names North Chicago in its account of the July 11 and 12, 2017 storm, when three to seven inches of rain fell across the area. A county survey afterward found 10 flood problem areas in North Chicago affecting 23 properties, including three sewer backups. The same plan reports that Waukegan had 106 flood problem areas affecting 185 properties from that storm, and that basement flooding made up more than half of the reports.

The Village of Lake Bluff's own stormwater master plan calls the July 2017 storm the storm of record, with more than seven inches in 12 hours, and notes that the village sits on mostly high-clay soil. The village's stormwater guidance says a sump pump needs a source of backup power, from a battery or a generator, and that downspouts should point away from structures.

We found no published North Chicago rule on sump discharge or downspouts, so ask the city building department before you change where a pump drains. The sewer provider, the North Shore Water Reclamation District, states that its sanitary and storm sewers are separate systems. That is one more reason to confirm which side a sump line may use.
