UV Leak Detection Light: What It Finds in North Chicago

A UV leak detection light is a handheld lamp that makes special fluorescent dye glow, so a plumber can see exactly where a slow leak starts. In North Chicago, IL homes, it is most useful for drain, toilet, and fixture leaks you can reach with dye, and much less useful for a pressurized pipe sealed inside a wall.

This guide explains how the light works, what it finds, what it cannot find, and what to try first. If you have water on the floor now, close the nearest shutoff and call. We are open 24/7.

1

What the light and the dye actually do

The method has two parts. First, a plumber adds a small amount of fluorescent dye to the water or the system being tested. Second, they darken the room and sweep a UV lamp across the suspect area. Any place the dye escapes glows bright yellow or green, even when the leak is too small to see in normal light.

The dye does not find a leak by itself. It follows water. That is why UV detection works best when the dye can travel the same path as the leak and when the escaping fluid reaches a surface you can see and light. Think of it as a tracer, not a scanner.

We would rather tell you that UV is the wrong tool than sell it to you. When the leak is in a pressurized pipe behind drywall, we choose from other methods and explain why. Our leak detection service covers the whole menu, so the method fits the problem.

3

Try these checks before anyone arrives

  1. 1

    Run the meter test

    Turn off every faucet and appliance. Look at your water meter. If the small dial or the flow indicator still moves, water is going somewhere.

  2. 2

    Do the toilet dye test yourself

    A few drops of food coloring in the tank, then wait fifteen minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking.

  3. 3

    Look under every sink and behind the appliances

    Use a flashlight and a dry paper towel on the traps, the supply lines, and the dishwasher and washer hoses.

  4. 4

    Check the water heater base

    A damp pan, rust streaks, or a puddle suggests a fitting or the tank itself. Note it but do not touch electric connections with wet hands.

  5. 5

    Mark the stain

    If a ceiling or wall has a stain, outline it in pencil and date it. If it grows, the leak is active, and that is useful information for the plumber.

If the meter still moves after those checks and you cannot find a wet spot, the leak is likely hidden. That is the time to call, because a slow leak behind a wall can rot framing and grow mold long before the water bill shows it.

4

What a dye and light visit looks like

The usual order of work

  • 1Interview. We ask where you saw water, when it appears, and whether it follows rain, laundry, or a flush. Patterns point to the source faster than tools do.
  • 2Dry-out and baseline. We wipe the area dry and shine the lamp first, so old stains and fresh dye are not confused.
  • 3Dye in the suspect system. The dye goes into the toilet tank, the drain, or the line we are tracing, and we wait long enough for it to travel.
  • 4Darkened sweep. With the room dim, we scan joints, seams, and the floor around fixtures. A glow marks the exit point.
  • 5Report and repair plan. You get a plain explanation of what we saw, what it means, and what the fix involves, with the price stated before work begins.

The dye is only a clue. A glowing joint tells us where water escapes, but the cause may sit upstream, such as a loose connection, a cracked fitting, or pressure that is too high. The repair should address that cause, not only the visible drip.

5

When a leak becomes an emergency

A drip under a sink is a repair. Water spraying from a pipe, a ceiling that is sagging, or a wet floor near electrical outlets is an emergency. Close the main shutoff, usually near the water meter or where the line enters the house, and call. Our burst pipe repair team works around the clock for this.

Once the flow is stopped, UV dye and other tools can help find the original failure point so the repair is complete. A quick patch without a diagnosis tends to come back at the next weak joint.

What Lake Forest and Gurnee say about sump pumps, sewer connections, and flooding

Lake Forest requires a Home Sewer Inspection when water billing is transferred on the sale of a single-family home or duplex. The inspection checks downspouts, sump pumps, and yard drains for unauthorized connections to the sanitary sewer. The city lists a fee for the inspection and one for a re-inspection, and it says permits are needed before correction work.

The Lake County Stormwater Management Commission reports that the July 2017 storm put about four inches of rain on Lake Forest in four hours at its peak.

Gurnee's village page says that a gravity sewer backup or a sump pump failure will likely lead to basement flooding, and it encourages residents to install backup systems. The village names the low-lying area around the Des Plaines River and its tributaries as its most dangerous flood hazard area.

A burst of rain like that is when a downspout or sump line tied to the wrong pipe shows itself.

For a home in the city, a dye trace can show where a questionable drain line ends before the inspector arrives, and a wet basement after a storm can be sorted into groundwater seepage or a plumbing leak by testing the plumbing side.
