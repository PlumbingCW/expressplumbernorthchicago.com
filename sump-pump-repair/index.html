Water in a sump pit during a storm is a clock, not a nuisance. Our phones are answered 24/7, and storm nights are exactly when we expect you to call. Pit full and pump silent: your first ten minutes

A sump pump that will not run while water climbs toward the rim of the pit needs attention the same hour. Start with the simple causes, because they account for a surprising share of calls.

Safe checks before we get there

  • 1Look at the power cord. Pumps are often plugged into a GFCI outlet that trips during storms, so press the reset button.
  • 2Lift the float arm gently with a stick. If the pump starts, the float was stuck against the wall of the pit, not the pump itself.
  • 3Listen for a humming motor that does not move water. That points to a jammed impeller or a clogged intake.
  • 4If the pit is overflowing, bucket water into a utility sink or floor drain only if you can do it without standing in water near live power.

Never reach into a pit with the pump plugged in. If you are unsure, step back and call. We can often tell from your description which of these four it is.

3

Six ways a sump pump actually fails

Stuck float

Silt or the pump's own cord pins the float down. The motor never gets the signal.

Failed check valve

Water drains back into the pit after each cycle, so the pump short-cycles and wears out.

Burned motor

A pump that ran dry or ran nonstop in a long storm overheats. Replacement is the honest answer.

Clogged intake

Gravel, silt or debris keeps the impeller from moving water.

Frozen or blocked discharge

The pipe outside can ice over or get crushed, so water has nowhere to go.

No backup power

A storm that takes out the electric service also stops a pump with no battery or water-powered backup.

Ejector pumps, the sealed units that lift toilet and laundry waste from a basement bath, are a related but different repair. If one fails, wastewater has nowhere to go, so treat it like a sewer problem, the kind our sewer line cleaning crew sees, and call right away.

5

Discharge rules: ask before you redirect

Local note

Storm and sanitary sewers are separate here

The district that treats North Chicago's sewage reports that rainwater and wastewater travel in separate pipes, so nothing combines them. A sump pump moves groundwater and rain, so it belongs on the storm side or outdoors, not tied into the sanitary line. When a pump overloads the wrong pipe, the district's system pays for it and your basement can pay again.

We found no North Chicago rule posted online about where a sump pump may discharge. Call the building department (847-596-8650) before you move or add a discharge pipe, and we will work from what they say.

If your problem is seepage through walls and floor rather than a dead pump, a pump swap will not solve it. Our basement waterproofing page covers drains and sealing, and our plumbing installation crew can add a second pit or a battery backup.
